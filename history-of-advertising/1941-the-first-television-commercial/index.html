    1941. The first television commercial

    The $9 Bulova spot of 1 July 1941 created the paid TV unit. Why it took thirty years, a scandal and a tobacco ban to become the 30-second spot you buy today.

    The moment

    Tuesday, 1 July 1941, 2.29 in the afternoon. NBC’s New York station, WNBT, is about to carry a ball game from Ebbets Field, the Brooklyn Dodgers against the Philadelphia Phillies. Before the first pitch, the few thousand sets in the city show a clock face over a map of the United States, and the station announcer, Ray Forrest, reads the line Bulova has paid for: “America runs on Bulova time.” The bill is nine dollars, four for air time and five for station charges [5]. It runs about ten seconds. Then the ball game. That morning the Federal Communications Commission’s new rules had come into force, and television, after a decade of experiments, was allowed to sell.

    What came before

    Radio had already written the business model, and the unit of sale was the programme, whole. Advertisers, through their agencies, owned the shows outright. Sylvester “Pat” Weaver, who would later run NBC, began at Young and Rubicam making programmes for the agency’s clients, because that is where programmes were made [9].

    What held television back was the lack of an agreed technical standard. The National Television System Committee recommended 525 lines on 8 March 1941, the FCC approved it on 30 April and authorised commercial operation from 1 July, and on 2 May it licensed ten stations, WNBT holding licence number one [7].

    Bulova’s is called the first legal commercial because earlier experimental broadcasts had carried sponsor mentions nobody could be paid for. How long it ran is less settled than the date. Most accounts say ten seconds, others nine or twenty; Ad Age in 1995 described a “Bulova Time Check” with a ticking second hand, and the clock-over-a-map clip that circulates online is a reconstruction [5]. Then the war. From April 1942 to October 1945 the government halted production of new receivers, and in 1946, 8,000 American households owned a set.

    What it changed

    On 2 July 1941 a marketer could buy seconds of attention, by the clock, in front of a moving picture with sound, inside the home. That unit, the paid spot, is what the Bulova bill records. For the next fifteen years almost nobody bought it that way.

    Texaco Star Theater opened on NBC on 8 June 1948 with Milton Berle, when fewer than 2 percent of households had a set [8]. Households with television went from 172,000 in 1948 to 3.9 million in 1950, 30.7 million in 1955 and 52.7 million in 1965 [1].

    Television advertising was $58 million in 1949, 1.1 percent of all US advertising. By 1954 it was $809 million and had passed radio and magazines; by 1960 it was $1.6 billion and 13.6 percent of the total, second only to newspapers [2].

    But the thing being bought was still the programme. Most 1950s shows had a single underwriter, and, as the historian James Baughman puts it, “commercials and product plugs were routinely integrated into programs, including those for children” [12]. Weaver, now at NBC, set out to break that with the magazine concept: the network makes the programme and sells insertions to several advertisers, the way a magazine sells pages [9]. Today, from 14 January 1952, and Tonight, from 1954, were built as vehicles for it [10]. Erik Barnouw: “Now Weaver was determined that control should shift to the networks” [11].

    The quiz shows took the programme from the sponsors. The $64,000 Question went on CBS on 7 June 1955 for Revlon; Twenty-One followed on NBC for Geritol. When Charles Van Doren told a House subcommittee on 2 November 1959 that he had been fed the answers, the hearings exposed producers and sponsors rigging outcomes, Congress made fixing a crime in 1960, and the networks took programme control for themselves [14][15]. From then on the sponsor was a buyer of time, and the agency’s job on television was the spot.

    The last change was the length of the unit. Through the 1960s the standard commercial was sixty seconds. The cigarette ban took tobacco off the air from 1 January 1971, the last spot running at 11.50 that night during Johnny Carson’s programme, and tobacco had bought sixty-second units almost without exception [16]. The networks replaced the lost revenue by selling halves. The thirty-second spot became the standard.

    Eighty-five years on, the unit survives and the pipe has changed. In May 2025 streaming took 44.8 percent of time spent with the television screen, more than broadcast and cable combined [25]. Connected television advertising was $9.7 billion in 2020 and $23.6 billion in 2024 [26].

    The Mad Men thread

    The series runs from 1960 to 1970 inside a Madison Avenue agency, the years when the spot replaced the show.

    Rosser Reeves at Ted Bates made the hard-sell spot the template. His Anacin commercial, hammers pounding inside a drawn skull and the promise of fast relief, was a fifty-nine-second film that cost $8,400; Reeves said Anacin came to Bates at $18 million a year in sales, went to $54 million within eighteen months, and spent $86 million putting that one film on the air [13]. His rule for the medium puts the decade in one sentence: “The purpose of a commercial is not to entertain the viewer but to sell him” [12].

    Bill Bernbach at Doyle Dane Bernbach took the other road. Mark Tungate reads the industry as “this constant tug of war between two schools: the creatives, who believe art inspires consumers to buy, and the pragmatists, who sell based on facts” [17]. When DDB took Volkswagen to television it kept the print voice: one Beetle, one fact, a dry narrator, the 1964 “Snow Plow” spot asking how the man who drives the snowplough gets to the snowplough [23]. It is still the cleanest demonstration spot we know.

    Leo Burnett in Chicago turned Marlboro into the cowboy in the mid-1950s, and on television the cowboy rode to the theme from The Magnificent Seven until the ban took him off the air [22].

    David Ogilvy came to the medium late. The television chapter in Confessions of an Advertising Man, in 1963, is short and leans on borrowed research: commercials that set up a problem, “then prove the solution by demonstration, sell four times as many people as commercials which merely preach about the product” [18]. By 1983 he had rules of his own, ranking problem-solution, demonstration, testimonials and the derided talking head among the formats “found to be above average in their ability to change people’s brand preference” [19]. Twenty years in, his rules and Reeves’s had mostly converged; Ogilvy’s just had better manners.

    Stephen Fox opens The Mirror Makers with the medium’s permanent reputation: “Practically everyone dislikes it. Advertising interrupts radio and television programs” [20]. The interruption was the business.

    Two set pieces deserve the record. The series gives the Lucky Strike line “It’s toasted” to its lead in 1960. American Tobacco’s own history dates the first “IT’S TOASTED” newspaper ad to January 1917 and records that George Washington Hill retained Albert Lasker’s Lord and Thomas late in 1925, eight years after the line [21]. The Kodak Carousel pitch is the series’ invention: Kodak’s timeline records the first Carousel projector in 1961, and its agency for most of the century was J. Walter Thompson [24].

    Applied: if you are a B2C brand

    Say you sell a regional snack brand in four states and your media plan is social video and connected television. The record gives you one distinction, between attention you own and attention you rent. Most media plans we see at Brave New split the budget by channel and never by that line.

    Texaco owned Tuesday night; when the audience arrived, it arrived for Texaco. That is what a creator series with your name on it, or your own channel, is today. You fund the programme, you carry a bad season, and the audience is yours for as long as you keep making it. The quiz scandals are the warning that comes with ownership. The moment the sponsor’s thumb shows on the content, the audience leaves and the regulator arrives.

    Connected television is the Bulova unit. You rent fifteen or thirty seconds in someone else’s programme, and the record on how to spend them has not changed since Reeves and Ogilvy argued about it: one claim, one demonstration, the name early and often. The sixty-second spot died when the tobacco money left and the networks sold halves to fill the hole. So when a platform offers you six seconds, ask what the six replaces.

    Applied: if you are a B2B brand

    Say you sell cybersecurity to mid-size banks and someone proposes a branded video series: a chief information security officer interviewing peers, your logo on the title card. Start where Weaver started, with control of the programme.

    If you sponsor someone else’s series, you are the 1950s advertiser without the 1950s power. You get the adjacency and they keep the editorial. If you make the series, you are NBC in 1952: you fund it, you set the format, and the audience, a few hundred security and risk officers at the banks you want, is yours for as long as the programme is worth their time.

    The quiz-show lesson applies with more force here. Your buyers are paid to detect manipulation. A series in which every guest endorses your category, or every episode ends on your demo, is Twenty-One with the answers handed out in advance. Keep a wall between the people who sell and the people who book guests and cut episodes, and let a guest say something your sales team would rather they had not. It is the only reason the audience comes back.

    Measure it by who returns for the second episode. Berle’s audience in 1948 was tiny, and it bought the sets.

    What to do with it

    1. Add an ownership column to the media plan. For every line, mark whether you own the programme or rent the time, what it costs to keep owning it next quarter, and what you lose if you stop.
    2. Build the spot on one claim. State it in the first three seconds and demonstrate it once. Test the fifteen against the thirty on the same claim, and do not let the platform’s default length decide.
    3. If you fund a programme, write the Weaver rules before episode one: who sets the format, who books the guests, what the sponsor may never touch. Put them in the production contract.
    4. Run a quiz-show audit on any series you already have. Count the episodes where the outcome favoured you. If it is all of them, your audience has noticed.
    5. Price attention in Bulova units. Divide each channel’s spend by the seconds of attention it bought, and compare owned programme with rented spot on the same line. Keep the one whose cost per second falls as the audience grows.
