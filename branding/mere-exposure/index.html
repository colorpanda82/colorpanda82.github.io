    Mere exposure: familiarity is pleasant, and that is a problem

    Abstract

    Robert Zajonc showed in 1968 that repeated exposure to something increases liking for it, with no argument, no information and no awareness that it is happening [1]. It is among the most replicated findings in social psychology, confirmed across hundreds of experiments [2][3]. It is also the mechanism underneath almost all brand advertising that appears to say nothing. The work is not making a case. It is accumulating familiarity, which converts into preference at the moment of choice and is then explained as a judgement about quality. The same mechanism makes repeated falsehoods feel true, and a reference work that teaches the technique without saying so is doing half a job.

    Origin

    Zajonc published Attitudinal Effects of Mere Exposure as a monograph supplement to the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 1968 [1]. The problem he set out to test was old: whether familiarity breeds liking or contempt. The folk wisdom went both ways. The data did not.

    His experiments used stimuli with no prior meaning to the participants, nonsense words, Chinese characters shown to people who could not read them, photographs of strangers. He varied how often each was shown and then asked people how much they liked each one. Liking rose with exposure. His own statement of the finding is short: “mere repeated exposure of the individual to a stimulus is a sufficient condition for the enhancement of his attitude toward it” [1].

    The word “mere” was chosen with care. No reward was attached to the stimulus. No argument was made for it. It was simply there, more often.

    What the evidence shows

    The effect replicates. Bornstein’s 1989 meta-analysis covered 208 experiments and found the effect reliable, with the average effect size in the moderate range [2]. Montoya and colleagues’ 2017 meta-analysis, with a larger set of studies and a stricter method, confirmed it [3]. For a finding in social psychology, where the replication record of the last decade has been unkind, this is a strong position.

    It works without awareness. Kunst-Wilson and Zajonc in 1980 showed shapes for a few milliseconds, too briefly to be consciously recognised, and still found that participants preferred the shapes they had been shown [4]. People liked things they could not report having seen. That result is the one most relevant to advertising, and it has been replicated.

    It follows an inverted U. Both meta-analyses find that liking rises with exposure and then falls as exposure becomes excessive [2][3]. The peak varies with the stimulus and the spacing. Familiarity helps until it becomes tedium. This is the empirical basis for frequency caps, and most frequency caps are set by budget rather than by this curve.

    The same mechanism operates on belief. Hasher, Goldstein and Toppino showed in 1977 that statements repeated across sessions were rated as more likely to be true, regardless of whether they were [5]. The illusory truth effect is mere exposure applied to propositions. It has replicated as reliably as the original, and it is the part of this entry that marketing writing usually leaves out.

    The mechanism is fluency. The current explanation, set out most fully by Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman, is that repeated stimuli are processed more easily, that ease is experienced as a mild positive feeling, and that the feeling is attributed to the stimulus rather than to the ease [6]. Processing fluency has its own entry in this section. Mere exposure is its most studied case.

    The challenge

    The challenge to mere exposure is not to whether it exists. It is to scope and to interpretation.

    Scope. The effect is strongest for neutral or mildly positive stimuli. Repeated exposure to something initially disliked does not reliably produce liking, and can produce the opposite [2]. A brand that buyers already dislike does not fix that with frequency. This is the finding most often ignored by media plans that treat reach as a cure.

    Duration. The effect in the laboratory is measured minutes or days after exposure. How long it persists, and how it decays, is less settled. The advertising literature on memory decay, Binet and Field’s work on long and short effects among others, suggests brand memory built this way is durable, but that is an inference across two literatures rather than a single demonstrated chain [7].

    Interpretation. Some researchers hold that the effect is better explained by a reduction in uncertainty or threat than by fluency. The disagreement is about mechanism, not about the behavioural result, and a marketer does not need to resolve it. The result is the same either way.

    The literature here is unusually settled for the behaviour and still arguing about the cause. Report it that way.

    Where I see this go wrong

    The mechanism is denied. Brand advertising that says nothing is defended on the grounds that it says something subtle. Usually it does not. It is working, when it works, by mere exposure, and the pretence that there is a message in it leads to creative briefs that chase a message nobody will receive. Say what the work is doing and plan for that.

    The inverted U is ignored. Frequency is set by what the budget allows rather than by where liking peaks. Past the peak, every additional exposure is paid for and counterproductive. The two meta-analyses give the shape of the curve. Almost no media plan I have seen refers to it.

    The ethics are not stated. Mere exposure and illusory truth are the same mechanism. A brand that builds preference through repetition is using the machinery that makes repeated falsehoods feel true. That does not make brand advertising dishonest. It does mean the technique is indifferent to whether the thing being repeated is any good, and anyone using it should know that and say so. This section promised documented studies, and the documentation here cuts both ways.

    I came to marketing from television, and the thing television understood about repetition was that it was a cost to be minimised, not a mechanism to be used. The idea that showing the same thing again was itself the point, independent of what the thing said, was slower to arrive than the research that proved it.

    What to do with it

    1. Decide, in writing, whether a piece of brand work is making an argument or building familiarity. Most of it is the second. Plan the second honestly: distinctive assets, consistency, repetition, no message to test because there is no message.
    2. Set frequency from the curve, not the budget. Look at the shape in Bornstein and Montoya, estimate where your stimulus peaks, and cap there. Spend the remainder on reach.
    3. Do not use repetition to fix dislike. If buyers who know you do not like you, more exposure makes it worse. Fix the product or the service first.
    4. Keep the stimulus constant. Mere exposure requires the thing seen to be recognisably the same thing. Every refresh of the asset resets the count. See Distinctive brand assets.
    5. Separate the claims you repeat from the assets you repeat. Assets can be repeated indefinitely. Claims that are repeated will be believed whether or not they are true, which is a reason to make sure yours are.
    6. Read Zajonc 1968 and Hasher 1977 together, once. They are the same paper about two different things, and holding both in mind is the whole of the ethical position this entry takes.

    If you run the company rather than the marketing

    A large part of your brand budget is buying familiarity, not making an argument, and it is worth knowing that so you can judge it on the right terms.

    Work that says nothing is not necessarily failing. If it is recognisably you and it is seen often enough by the right people, it is doing what the evidence says repetition does, which is making you the comfortable choice when the buyer finally buys. Judge it on recognition and preference among category buyers, not on whether you personally find it persuasive. It was not built to persuade you.

    Two cautions. Repetition does not fix a product people dislike; it entrenches the dislike. And repetition makes claims feel true whether they are or not, which puts an obligation on you to be sure the claims are. The mechanism does not care. You should.

    Where this gets used

    Working out which parts of a brand plan are argument and which are familiarity, and funding the familiarity half with the frequency curve rather than the budget line, is part of a go-to-market retainer.
