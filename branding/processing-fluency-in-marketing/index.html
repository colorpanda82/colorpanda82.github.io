    Processing fluency in marketing: what is easy to read feels true

    Abstract

    Things that are easy to perceive, read, say and remember are judged more true, more likeable and more familiar than the same things made harder [1]. Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman set the mechanism out in 2004: the brain registers the ease of its own processing and misreads that ease as a property of the thing processed. The core effect replicates well. One popular offshoot, the claim that making things harder to read improves learning, did not [2]. For a brand the implication is the scientific case for consistency and against cleverness: every unit of effort you ask of the reader is charged to the brand's credibility.

    Origin

    The idea has a long tail in psychology, but the 2004 paper by Rolf Reber, Norbert Schwarz and Piotr Winkielman gave it a name and a theory [1]. Their title asked whether beauty is in the perceiver’s processing experience, and their answer was that a good part of it is. Stimuli that are processed more fluently, because they are symmetrical, familiar, high in contrast, or have been seen before, are experienced as more pleasant.

    The problem they addressed was that several separate findings looked like one thing. Mere exposure, where repetition produces liking. The illusory truth effect, where repetition produces belief. Preferences for symmetry and prototypicality. Their proposal was that all of these ran through a single mechanism: fluency feels good, and the feeling is attributed to the object.

    Their statement of it: “the more fluently perceivers can process an object, the more positive their aesthetic response” [1].

    What the evidence shows

    Fluency raises judgements of truth. Statements printed in high-contrast, easily read type are rated more likely to be true than identical statements in low-contrast type [3]. Rhyming aphorisms are judged more accurate than non-rhyming versions with the same meaning [4]. Neither the colour contrast nor the rhyme carries any information about truth. The ease does the work.

    Fluency raises liking and familiarity. Repeated stimuli are processed more easily and liked more, which is the mere exposure finding reinterpreted [5]. Prototypical faces and shapes, which are processed easily because they match a stored average, are preferred.

    Fluency lowers perceived effort and risk. Song and Schwarz showed in 2008 that a recipe printed in a hard-to-read font was judged to take longer and require more skill than the same recipe in a clear font [6]. Alter and Oppenheimer found in 2006 that stocks with pronounceable ticker symbols outperformed those with unpronounceable ones in the period after listing, a result they attributed to fluency making the company feel safer [7].

    The effect sits under most of this section. Mere exposure is fluency from repetition. The distinctive assets argument is fluency from consistency. The System 1 account is, in part, a description of fluent processing. This is the mechanism the others borrow, and it is the one with the best evidence.

    The challenge

    Two challenges, one to interpretation and one to an offshoot.

    Interpretation. Alter and Oppenheimer’s 2009 review, Uniting the Tribes of Fluency, argued that fluency is not one thing but several, perceptual, conceptual, linguistic, memory-based, which happen to produce similar downstream effects [8]. If that is right, then “fluency” is a family of mechanisms rather than a single one, and findings from one kind do not automatically transfer to another. A brand that is visually fluent is not thereby conceptually fluent. This is a refinement rather than a refutation, and the practical advice survives it.

    The offshoot that failed. In 2011 Diemand-Yauman, Oppenheimer and Vaughan reported that students learned material better when it was printed in a harder-to-read font, on the theory that disfluency triggered deeper processing [9]. The result was widely reported and widely applied. It did not replicate. Meyer and colleagues ran a pre-registered replication in 2015 across several studies and found no benefit from disfluent fonts, and a subsequent meta-analysis agreed [2]. The core fluency finding is intact. The claim that disfluency helps is not.

    This matters for the section because it is the clearest case of a popular marketing idea, “make them work for it”, resting on a finding that fell over when tested properly. The honest record is: fluency helps, disfluency does not help, and the second is not the mirror image of the first.

    Where I see this go wrong

    Cleverness is mistaken for quality. A headline that takes two readings to parse, a logo that needs explaining, a name that needs spelling out on the phone. Each is a small tax on fluency, and each is paid in credibility rather than in attention. The creative team experiences the difficulty as sophistication. The buyer experiences it as effort and attributes the effort to the brand.

    Consistency is treated as a design preference. The fluency evidence is the reason consistency works: the same asset, seen again, is processed more easily, and the ease is read as trust. Every variation resets the processing. Teams that redesign for freshness are, in fluency terms, choosing to be harder to process. See Distinctive brand assets.

    The disfluency idea is still quoted. I still see “make the reader slow down” as a creative principle, with the 2011 study behind it. The study did not replicate. The principle should go with it.

    Teaching AI marketing at the Bucharest University of Economic Studies, the fluency literature is the one I find students resist most, because it says that effort spent making the work interesting can be effort spent making it worse. That is an uncomfortable lesson for anyone who got into the field because they liked the clever work. It is still the lesson.

    What to do with it

    1. Audit every brand touchpoint for processing cost. Can the name be said, spelled and remembered on one hearing. Can the logo be recognised at a glance. Can the headline be understood on one reading. Each failure is a fluency tax.
    2. Keep the assets fixed. Fluency from familiarity requires the thing to be familiar, which requires it to be the same thing. See Distinctive brand assets for the test.
    3. Prefer high contrast, legible type, short sentences and plain names in anything a buyer has to process quickly. The evidence is that these are read as more trustworthy, and not only as more accessible.
    4. Stop asking buyers to work for it. If a creative route depends on the reader slowing down, the finding it relies on failed to replicate, and the route is spending credibility to buy attention.
    5. Test fluency directly. Show the asset or the claim briefly and ask for a liking or truth rating. Compare against a less fluent version of the same thing. The effect is large enough to see in a small sample.
    6. Treat fluency as a family, not a switch. Visual fluency, verbal fluency and conceptual fluency are different jobs. A brand can be easy to see and hard to understand. Check each.

    If you run the company rather than the marketing

    The finding here is simple enough to apply from the top. Anything that is hard to read, say, spell or recognise is costing you trust, and the cost is paid by the brand rather than noticed by the reader.

    When you are shown a new name, a new mark, a new line, ask the dull question. Can I say it, spell it and remember it after hearing it once. Can a buyer recognise the mark in the time it takes to scroll past it. If the answer is no, the creative team will tell you the difficulty is distinctive. The evidence says the difficulty is read as untrustworthiness and attributed to you.

    One more. If anyone proposes making the work harder to process on purpose, to make people slow down and engage, know that the study behind that idea failed to replicate in 2015 and the field has moved on. It is still quoted. It should not be funded.

    Where this gets used

    Scoring a brand's assets and copy for processing cost, and finding the places where cleverness is quietly charging the brand for attention, is part of every brand consolidation sprint.
