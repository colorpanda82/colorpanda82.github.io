    What is answer engine optimization (AEO)?

    Also known as
    AEO, answer engine optimization

    Answer engine optimization, or AEO, is the practice of preparing content so that it is used in a direct answer rather than in a list of links. In practice it means answering one question per page, plainly and early, in a block that can be lifted out whole and still make sense.

    Where the term comes from, which is nowhere in particular

    AEO has no founding paper. It is a trade term, and it predates the current wave of AI assistants: people were using it for featured snippets and voice search results years before a language model was involved.

    That history is useful rather than embarrassing. The featured snippet era already established the core mechanic, which is that a page can win the answer and lose the visit. Everything happening now is a harder version of the same trade.

    How it differs from GEO, since the two are used interchangeably and should not be

    They overlap, and the distinction is still worth holding.

    GEO is about being part of the material a generated answer is synthesised from, often alongside several other sources, with or without attribution.

    AEO is about being the answer. One question, one page, one extractable block. It applies to featured snippets, voice assistants and AI overviews as much as to chat assistants.

    In daily work the techniques converge. The reason to keep them apart is diagnostic: if your problem is that nobody cites you, that is a GEO problem. If your problem is that you are cited but the answer given is thin or wrong, that is an AEO problem, and the fix is in how your page is structured rather than in whether it is found.

    What an answer-shaped page looks like

    Six things, none of them complicated.

    The question is the heading, phrased the way a person would ask it rather than the way a marketer would title it. The answer is the first thing after it, in under about fifty words, before any context or qualification. One page answers one question. Headings are questions too, so that someone scanning gets the argument. Numbers carry their source and their date inline. And the page does not open by explaining what it is about to do.

    That last one is the most commonly broken. A page that opens “in this article we will explore” has spent its extractable block on nothing.

    The uncomfortable trade

    Winning the answer often means losing the visit. The reader gets what they came for and does not click.

    Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling. The honest case for doing it anyway is that the alternative is not “they visit your site instead”, it is “they get the answer from somebody else and never learn your name”. Being the uncredited answer is bad. Being absent is worse. A named citation in an answer is worth more than it looks, because it reaches people at the moment they are deciding who knows what they are talking about.

    That calculation changes by business. If your revenue depends on ad impressions on your own pages, losing the visit is a direct loss. If it depends on being known by people who will buy something considered and expensive later, it is mostly a gain.

    What to do with it

    1. One question per page. If a page answers three, it will win none of them.
    2. Put the answer first, under fifty words, before context. Write it so it survives being read alone.
    3. Make every H2 a question somebody would actually type or say.
    4. Attach dates to figures. An undated number is unusable in an answer and a careful engine will skip it.
    5. Decide, deliberately, which pages you are willing to lose the click on. Not every page should be answer-shaped.

    If you run the company rather than the marketing

    There is a decision here that belongs to you rather than to marketing, and it is usually made by default.

    Answer-shaped content trades traffic for presence. You will appear in more answers and get fewer visits. Whether that is a good trade depends entirely on how your business makes money, and nobody will ask you the question before reorganising the website around it.

    If you sell something considered, expensive and infrequent, where buyers spend months forming a shortlist before they ever contact anyone, the trade is almost certainly worth it. Being the name that comes up when a buyer asks a neutral party is worth more than a session on your site that nobody can attribute to revenue anyway.

    If your model depends on traffic to your own pages, it is a worse trade and should be made selectively, page by page.

    Either way, make it once, explicitly, rather than discovering it a year later in a traffic report.
