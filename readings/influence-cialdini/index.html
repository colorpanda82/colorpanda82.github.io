    Influence. Robert Cialdini (1984)

    The book that catalogued the mechanisms of persuasion into six principles, built on three years of fieldwork inside sales training, fundraising and advertising operations. It is the most useful book on this list and the most misused.

    Anne Becheru, Brave NewSept 2026 · 4 min read · Updated 8 Oct 2026

    What it argues

    That compliance professionals, the people whose living depends on getting others to say yes, have converged on a small set of techniques that work because they exploit shortcuts people rely on to decide quickly.

    The six, in Cialdini’s original formulation: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority and scarcity. A seventh, unity, was added in later work.

    The method is what makes it credible. Cialdini spent three years working inside the organisations that use these techniques, which is participant observation rather than laboratory work, and the principles were then tested experimentally.

    What it gets right

    The principles are real, they are supported by experimental evidence, and several have replicated well. Social proof and authority in particular are about as solid as findings in social psychology get.

    The framing is also honest in a way the imitators are not. Cialdini wrote the book partly as a defence manual, so that ordinary people could recognise when these techniques were being used on them. That intent is visible throughout and it is the reason the book has aged better than the genre it created.

    What goes wrong with it

    Almost everything downstream.

    This is the most misapplied book in marketing. The principles get deployed as tactics without the conditions that make them work, which produces the fake countdown timer, the invented scarcity, the “17 people are viewing this” notice. Cialdini has been consistently clear that manufactured social proof and false scarcity are both unethical and, over time, ineffective, because they train people to discount the signal.

    There is a deeper tension worth naming. Scarcity, one of the six, is in direct conflict with the availability argument in Sharp: make it harder to get versus make it as easy to buy as possible. Both have evidence behind them. The resolution is that they operate at different moments and in different categories, and anyone applying scarcity in a category where customers already experience you as unreliable is adding to a problem rather than creating desire.

    Some of the specific studies in the original have also been affected by the wider replication problems in social psychology. The core principles hold; the individual anecdotes should not be quoted as though each were separately bulletproof.

    Who should read it, and who should not

    Read it to recognise what is being done to you. That was the author’s stated purpose and it remains the best reason.

    Read it if you write offers or sales copy, with the ethical sections included rather than skipped.

    Do not hand it to a sales team as a tactics manual. That is how you end up with fake urgency and a reputation problem.

    The soundbite

    Six mechanisms that work, written partly so you could tell when they were being used on you, and now mostly used on you.

    Sources

    1. Cialdini, R. B. (1984). Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion. First edition; revised and expanded in later editions, including Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion, New and Expanded, 2021, which adds unity as a seventh principle.
    2. Cialdini, R. B. (2016). Pre-Suasion. On the moment before the message, and arguably the more original book.

    If you run the company rather than the marketing

    There is a governance question in this book and it is worth taking seriously.

    The six principles work. They work whether or not what you are selling is good, which means they are available to your team as a way of hitting a number without improving anything. The techniques that cross the line, invented scarcity, manufactured social proof, false deadlines, are cheap, effective in the short run, and almost never explicitly authorised. They appear because somebody is under pressure.

    So the useful move is to decide, in advance and in writing, which of these you will not use. Specifically: no countdown that resets, no scarcity claim that is not true, no review or testimonial that was not freely given, no “recommended by” that was paid for without disclosure.

    That list costs you a little conversion. It also means that when a buyer checks one of your claims, and in a considered purchase somebody always does, the claim holds. That is worth more than the conversion, and it is the kind of decision that only gets made at your level before it is needed.

    Where this gets used

    Deciding in writing which persuasion techniques a company will not use, before anyone is under pressure, is part of the governance work in a brand consolidation sprint.
