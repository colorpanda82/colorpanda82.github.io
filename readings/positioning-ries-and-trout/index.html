    Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind. Al Ries and Jack Trout (1981)

    The book that moved the battleground from the product to the prospect's mind. Own a word, or own a place relative to the leader, before somebody else does. It began as a series of articles in Advertising Age in 1972 and has shaped how marketers talk ever since.

    What it argues

    That the mind is overloaded and defends itself by simplifying. Buyers hold a short mental ladder per category, three or four names, and a brand’s position is its rung.

    From that, three moves survive.

    You cannot beat a leader by being a better version of them, because the rung is occupied. The first brand into a category has a structural advantage in memory that is separate from any advantage in the product. And repositioning the competition is often more achievable than repositioning yourself.

    The second of these is the one that still does the most work, and it is a claim about memory rather than about quality.

    What it gets right

    The central insight is correct and was genuinely new: that the contest happens in the buyer’s head rather than in the product specification, and that memory is the scarce resource.

    It is also the book that taught marketers to think about the competitive set from the buyer’s point of view rather than from the industry’s. That reframing is permanent and is now so ordinary that the book gets underrated for introducing it.

    What it is wrong about

    It predates the empirical work on how little buyers actually differentiate between brands, and it assumes positioning is more controllable than the evidence supports.

    The Ehrenberg-Bass position, set out most directly by Romaniuk, is that most buyers in most categories perceive little meaningful difference and do not go looking for one. If that holds in your category, then “own a word” describes an ambition rather than a mechanism, and the achievable goal is to be recognised rather than to be meaningfully distinct.

    This is the sharpest live disagreement in branding and the honest answer is that both are doing work at different moments. Position for the small number actively deciding. Be recognisable for everyone else.

    The book is also written with enormous confidence and no data, which is worth noticing in a field that now has some.

    Who should read it, and who should not

    Read it for the reframing, which is short and permanent.

    Read it before Romaniuk, not after. The argument only makes sense in that order.

    Do not build a strategy on it alone, and be careful with it in low-interest categories, where the premise that buyers are comparing you at all may simply be false.

    The soundbite

    You do not position a product. You position it in a mind that is already full.

    Sources

    1. Ries, A. and Trout, J. (1981). Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind. McGraw-Hill. First published as articles in Advertising Age, 1972.
    2. Romaniuk, J. (2018). Building Distinctive Brand Assets. Oxford University Press. The strongest counter-position.

    If you run the company rather than the marketing

    The reason this book matters to you is that it describes a constraint you cannot spend your way out of.

    A buyer holds a very short list per category. Not a long one. If you are not on it, you are not in the deal, and no amount of improving the product changes that on its own.

    What the book does not tell you, because it predates the evidence, is how little room there is to move. Buyers do not hold brands apart as finely as the positioning tradition assumes, and the attempt to own an abstract word, trust, innovation, partnership, is where most of this work dies. Everyone claims those.

    The version I would act on: decide what you want to be the obvious choice for, make it narrow enough to be plausible, and then say it the same way for years. The narrowing is the hard part, and it is yours to do.

    Where this gets used

    Narrowing what a company wants to be the obvious choice for, to something specific enough to be believed, is the work of a brand consolidation sprint.
