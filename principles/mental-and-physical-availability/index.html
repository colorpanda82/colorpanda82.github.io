    Mental availability and physical availability

    Abstract

    Sharp's claim is that brands grow by being easy to think of and easy to buy, because loyalty follows market share rather than causing it [1]. The second half is well tested: double jeopardy holds in 32 differentiated replications, in cars, business banking, aircraft and packaged goods [6][9][10]. The first half, that mental availability itself moves share, rests mostly on one institute. Practitioners should track penetration before any loyalty metric, stop expecting retention schemes to beat a law, and build memory links to buying situations rather than an awareness score.

    Origin

    Byron Sharp published How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don’t Know with Oxford University Press in 2010 [1]. Chapter 12 carries the two terms. Mental availability is the probability that buyers “notice, recognize and/or think of a brand in buying situations” [1][2]. Physical availability is the ease of finding and buying it. The book’s argument is that these two are what budgets should build. What “easy to buy” means has moved since 2010: in McKinsey’s 2024 B2B Pulse survey of nearly 4,000 decision makers in 13 countries, buyers used an average of ten channels to interact with suppliers, up from five in 2016, and among sellers that offer e-commerce it now brings in 34% of revenue against 17% from in-person sales [30].

    The problem Sharp was solving was a decade of loyalty-first management. Reichheld and Sasser had told Harvard Business Review readers in 1990 that “companies can boost profits by almost 100% by retaining just 5% more of their customers” [18], and Aaker’s Managing Brand Equity (1991) had put brand loyalty first among five equity assets [20]. Sharp’s answer: brands do not grow by deepening loyalty, they grow by adding buyers.

    The regularity underneath the book is double jeopardy, which William McPhee described in Formal Theories of Mass Behavior (1963) for actors, comic strips and radio presenters before anyone applied it to brands [5][9]. Ehrenberg, Goodhardt and Barwise formalised it for buying in the Journal of Marketing in 1990: “a small brand typically has far fewer buyers than a larger brand. In addition, its buyers tend to buy it less often” [6]. Uncles, Ehrenberg and Hammond (1995) tied the regularities together in the NBD-Dirichlet model [7]. Sharp’s own step before the book was brand salience, defined with Jenni Romaniuk in 2004 as “the brand’s propensity to be noticed or come to mind in buying situations” [8]. Mental availability is that idea renamed and widened. How Brands Grow Part 2 (2016) took the laws to emerging markets, services and luxury [3]; Romaniuk’s Building Distinctive Brand Assets (2018) is the operating manual for the mental half [4].

    What the evidence shows

    Ehrenberg, Goodhardt and Barwise (1990) reviewed the panel evidence, the theory and the known exceptions [6]. Graham, Bennett, Franke, Henfrey and Nagy-Hamada (2017) took the law into long-run continuous buying, emerging markets, capital purchasing and house-of-brands portfolios. Across 32 differentiated replications, some 35 years apart, they “find no boundary condition to the operation of the Double Jeopardy characteristic” [10].

    The B2B evidence is the part a Brave New reader will want. Romaniuk, Dawes and Faghidno (2021) published an Ehrenberg-Bass report with new survey data on UK business banking and US business insurance, plus a table of eight earlier B2B studies [9]. In banking, Barclays, used by 41% of firms surveyed, held 5.7 of 17 possible products per customer and 27% solely loyal customers; Metro, used by 11%, held 3.2 products and 3% [9]. The report does not state its sample size.

    The companion report in the same series asked 609 UK business banking buyers and 616 US business insurance buyers a second question, whether they would refuse to use each brand, and found the barrier to acquisition lies elsewhere: rejection averaged 11% across 22 UK banks and 7% across 17 US insurers, while the share of buyers unaware of a bank ran from 16% for Barclays to 80% for Handelsbanken [28]. A small B2B brand is small because most potential buyers do not know it, not because they have decided against it.

    The durable B2B case is aircraft. Bennett, Anesbury and Graham (2018) used 9,000 purchase records from 51 airlines over ten years: Boeing, bought by 86% of airlines, averaged 101 purchases per buyer; Embraer, bought by 16%, averaged 37 [9][31].

    Kim, Singh and Winer (2017) used A.C. Nielsen’s US household panel, 2004 to 2009, and measured the share of each brand’s dollar sales delivered by its top 20% of buyers across 238 brands in 22 categories, roughly 18,000 households [12]. The mean Pareto ratio was 0.73 with a standard deviation of 0.07; category averages ran from 0.64 for liquid detergent to 0.89 for cigarettes, and under Nielsen’s narrower brand definition the mean fell to 0.65 [12]. They note the rule “is not precisely .80” [12].

    Sharp and Graham read similar data differently. On Kantar data for about 600 UK brands in 40 categories, the top 20% deliver around 40% of sales in a quarter, “over 50%” in a year and “just over 60%” over five years, and 80% of a brand’s buyers make five purchases or fewer in five years [13]. Both figures sit well below 80; the gap is windows and brand definitions.

    The evidence for mental availability as a cause, rather than for double jeopardy as a pattern, is thinner. Romaniuk and Sharp (2004) set out the construct and a multi-cue measure [8]. Keller’s customer-based brand equity model (2001) had already put brand salience at the base of its pyramid: salience “influences the likelihood that the brand will be a member of the consideration set”, with depth (ease of recall) and breadth (the range of situations in which the brand comes to mind) [21]. Breadth is mental availability under another name. Keller then builds meaning, feelings and resonance on top; Sharp treats salience plus distribution as the whole job.

    Binet and Field’s meta-analysis of 996 IPA Databank campaigns (2013) lands on Sharp’s side for reach and elsewhere for mechanism. Their fame campaigns turned excess share of voice into growth at an annualised efficiency of 1.4 against 0.3 for other campaigns [23]. Reach is common ground. The mechanism is not: Sharp’s account is memory refreshed, Binet and Field’s is emotional priming that builds over years [23].

    The challenge

    Three serious critiques and one loud one.

    The first is the loyalty camp. Reichheld’s numbers are claims from company casework, not panel findings; The Loyalty Effect (1996) says “even a small improvement in customer retention can double profits” [18][19]. Aaker’s pyramid is a theorist’s structure [20]. Neither has met double jeopardy on the same data and won.

    The second is the academic critique of the Dirichlet’s generality, and it comes from inside the model. Fader and Schmittlein (1993) showed across product markets in Japan and the United States that high-share brands have significantly more loyalty than the Dirichlet predicts, and traced it to consumer segments created partly by distribution [14]. Jung, Gruca and Rego (2010) replicated this across US packaged goods and found excess loyalty positively related to market share in 86% of categories, a pattern some authors call triple jeopardy [15]. Scriven, Bound and Graham (2017) sorted the recurring deviations in 62 FMCG categories into three types and concluded that “brand performance remains close to Dirichlet prediction” [16]. The model bends, it does not break, and when it bends it favours the big brand.

    The third is scope. Sharp, Wright and Goodhardt (2002) themselves split repeat-purchase markets into repertoire markets, where the familiar patterns hold, and subscription markets such as insurance and banking, where most buyers use one brand at a time. The distinction “turns out to be an important boundary condition for some well-established generalisations”, and their analysis “does not extend to markets for durable products” [11]. The B2B evidence since then is real but narrower than the FMCG base: two Ehrenberg-Bass surveys with unstated samples, one aircraft dataset, and eight older studies, six from before 2001 [9]. Double jeopardy travels. The full Dirichlet toolkit, and the claim that availability is the only lever worth funding, have been tested far less outside the supermarket. Formisano, Pauwels and Zarantonello (2020) put it this way: marketers should “not oversimplify Dirichlet evidences by thinking of availability as the only (costly) response to all marketing challenges” [17].

    The loud one is Mark Ritson’s 2018 Marketing Week column: Sharp’s claim that it is “impossible” to value brand perceptions “is not just mistaken, it is a rejection of true scientific method” [27]. It added no data.

    We could not confirm whether the 16th edition of Kotler, Keller and Chernev’s Marketing Management (2021) covers double jeopardy or the Dirichlet at all [22]. Cialdini did not address mental availability. The availability heuristic the term echoes is Tversky and Kahneman’s (1973), a rule about judging frequency from ease of recall [25]. The nearest Cialdini gets is Pre-Suasion (2016), chapters 2 and 3, on attention and the principle that “what’s focal is causal”; that is attention in the moment of persuasion, not a brand’s standing in memory across buying situations [24].

    Where practitioners get it wrong

    Awareness is reported as mental availability. Sharp’s 2011 note is blunt: recall and recognition scores are “flawed by the use of a single, a-situational, cue”, usually the category name [2]. A brand can post high prompted awareness and still be absent from memory when a buyer needs a supplier for a specific job. The measure Romaniuk and Sharp propose uses one cue per buying situation [8].

    Small brands are given loyalty KPIs. Double jeopardy says a 10% penetration brand will show lower loyalty than a 40% brand for no reason other than size [6][9]. A retention target set at the leader’s number is one the law forbids. The loyalty-programme test in this literature is Sharp and Sharp (1997), which benchmarked a programme’s repeat-purchase patterns against Dirichlet norms [26].

    The 80/20 slide. Kim, Singh and Winer’s mean is 0.73 over six years; Sharp and Graham’s is around 0.40 in a quarter [12][13]. A plan that concentrates budget on the top fifth rests on a number the panel data does not produce.

    Our own observation. In one of the supply chain group’s established markets the brand had gone years without consistent presence and had slipped out of the consideration set, with the operation intact and the alternatives long-standing. Nothing about the service had changed. Its availability in buyers’ memory had. Rebuilding it was a matter of presence, a weekly publishing rhythm per market, and the clearest lesson of the year was that consistency of spend mattered more than size of spend. Fragmented allocation stopped campaigns before they could compound. The industry survey closest to that experience is Edelman and LinkedIn’s 2024 study of 3,484 executives in seven countries: 75% said a piece of thought leadership had sent them to research a product or service they had not been considering, and 86% of decision-makers said they would invite a company that consistently publishes good thought leadership into an RFP [29]. Presence gets a brand into the room; nothing else in the survey does.

    What to do with it

    1. Put penetration, the share of category buyers who bought you in the period, on the first line of every brand dashboard, with loyalty metrics beneath it [6][9].
    2. Before setting a retention or share-of-wallet target, tabulate the category by penetration as Romaniuk, Dawes and Faghidno describe, and set the target where the double jeopardy line predicts for your size [9].
    3. Replace the single-cue awareness question with a battery of buying situations, ask which brands come to mind for each, and report the share of situations in which you are named [8].
    4. Classify your category as repertoire or subscription before choosing metrics; use purchase frequency and share of requirements in the first, defection and tenure in the second [11].
    5. Test the Pareto ratio on your own customer file at one quarter, one year and three years, and stop using any figure you did not compute [12][13].
    6. List every place and moment a buyer could look for you, mark each yes or no, and fix the noes before spending on anything else in this article.
